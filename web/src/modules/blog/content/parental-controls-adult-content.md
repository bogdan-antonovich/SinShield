You cannot guarantee that a child will never encounter adult content, but you can remove many of the easiest routes and make it much more likely that they will tell you when something gets through. The effective setup combines a real child account, device restrictions, search and website filters, app review, and a calm conversation that does not make accidental exposure feel like an offense to hide.

Filters matter, but they are not the whole safety plan. The American Academy of Pediatrics says it could not find research proving that filters prevent pornography exposure, while still recommending adult-content blocks as preferable to leaving a child's device open to everything; it also stresses that [parents should not blame or shame a child who reports exposure](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/talking-to-your-child-about-online-pornography-exposure/).

> [!NOTE] The strongest starting setup
> Give the child their own age-correct account, manage it through Apple Family Sharing or Google Family Link, restrict adult websites and app installation, lock safer search, and tell the child they will not be punished for bringing you an upsetting image.

## 1. Use a child account instead of sharing yours

Parental controls work best when the operating system knows which account belongs to the child. A shared adult account can carry adult search history, purchases, messages, permissions, and settings into the child's experience, while making age-based controls harder to manage cleanly.

On Apple devices, add the child's Apple Account to Family Sharing and manage Screen Time from the parent or guardian account. On Android, use a supervised Google Account with Family Link; Google notes that iPhones and ordinary computers cannot be supervised as devices through Family Link in the same way as Android and Chromebook, even though some Chrome account controls may still apply.

Use a separate parent passcode and keep recovery information current. Do not give the child the unrestricted administrator account merely because repeated permission requests are inconvenient.

## 2. Turn on the strongest appropriate website setting

Apple's current [iPhone and iPad parental-control guide](https://support.apple.com/en-us/105121) provides adult-website limiting and an approved-websites-only option under the child's Apps & Websites restrictions. An allow-only mode is more restrictive and may fit a younger child, while an adult-site filter plus manual allow and block lists may fit an older child who needs broader research access.

In Family Link, Google's [Chrome controls](https://support.google.com/families/answer/7087030) offer **Try to block explicit sites** and **Only allow approved sites**, along with manual site lists and parent approval requests. Google explicitly warns that no filter is perfect, so test ordinary school sites and correct both missed blocks and false positives.

## 3. Lock search filtering without mistaking it for web filtering

For supervised Google Accounts, SafeSearch filtering is on and locked by default, subject to Google's age and region rules. SafeSearch filters detected explicit results in Google Search, but [Google states that it does not affect other search engines or sites opened directly](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/510).

Keep it on, then close the routes around it: restrict alternative search apps or browsers where appropriate, use the operating system's web-content controls, and review image- or AI-generation services separately. A clean search-results page does not mean the whole device is filtered.

## 4. Control app installation and review the apps already present

Preventing a child from installing an unrestricted browser is as important as configuring the main one. Apple offers Ask to Buy and app installation restrictions, while Family Link can approve, block, and set limits for apps on supervised Android devices.

Review social, messaging, file-sharing, private-browser, AI, and community apps based on the child's age and needs. Do not assume an app-store age rating means that every user-generated post is reviewed before display, and do not install covert surveillance when a simpler restriction and open agreement can do the job.

## 5. Add a network layer for known adult domains

An adult-filtering DNS resolver on the home router can block many known adult domains for devices using home Wi-Fi. Cloudflare's official documentation lists a [malware-and-adult-content resolver](https://developers.cloudflare.com/1.1.1.1/setup/) using `1.1.1.3` and `1.0.0.3`, but other reputable family DNS providers exist.

DNS is only one layer. It may not follow the phone onto mobile data, it can misclassify domains, and it cannot remove one explicit post from an otherwise allowed social platform, so keep device controls active and test each connection type.

## 6. Review settings together and check them after updates

Parental-control menus, age defaults, and supported features change. Schedule a short monthly check and another after a major operating-system update: confirm that the child account is still supervised, website restrictions remain active, new browsers have not appeared, search filtering is locked, and ordinary school or support resources still work.

Explain what you are checking and why, in language appropriate to the child's age. UNICEF notes that parental controls can protect children while also affecting privacy, and its [privacy guidance for parents](https://www.unicef.org/parenting/child-care/online-privacy) recommends reviewing device and app privacy settings as well as content and screen-time controls.

As children mature, involve them in changing the boundary. The objective is not permanent secret monitoring; it is age-appropriate safety and the gradual ability to navigate online spaces responsibly.

## 7. Give your child a safe response when something gets through

Tell them what adult content is in simple, age-appropriate language before the first disclosure. A useful rule is: close it, move away, and tell a trusted adult; you are not in trouble for an image that appeared unexpectedly.

When a child reports exposure, regulate your reaction first. Thank them for telling you, ask how it appeared and whether anyone sent or requested an image, preserve evidence only when necessary for safety or reporting, then close the technical route without interrogating or shaming them.

If an adult or peer has requested sexual images, threatened the child, sent targeted sexual material, or attempted sexual contact, treat that as a safeguarding issue rather than only a filter failure. Preserve relevant evidence, stop contact where safe, and use the appropriate local child-protection or law-enforcement reporting channel.

## Choose controls that match age and risk

The AAP describes parental controls as tools that work better alongside positive communication and relationship-building. Its [guidance on media limits](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/parental-controls-for-kids-setting-safe-and-healthy-media-limits.aspx) recommends discussion, co-use, and a family media plan rather than rules alone.

- **Younger child:** start with approved sites and apps only, purchases requiring approval, and use in shared spaces.
- **Older child beginning independent use:** keep adult-site and search filters, discuss user-generated content, and review new accounts together.
- **Teen:** retain necessary safety boundaries, explain any monitoring, respect reasonable privacy, and build a path toward more autonomy.
- **Known high-risk situation:** involve a pediatrician or qualified mental-health or safeguarding professional rather than escalating secret surveillance without support.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can parental controls block all pornography?

No. Filters can miss content, explicit material can appear inside allowed platforms, and settings can fail or be changed, so use several layers and maintain a safe disclosure path.

### Is Apple Screen Time enough?

It is a strong first layer when correctly configured, especially with a separate parent passcode. It still needs app review, safer search, regular checks, and conversation.

### Is Google Family Link available on iPhone?

A parent can run the Family Link app on iPhone, and a child's managed Google Account can receive some Chrome controls there, but Google says iPhones cannot be supervised as devices through Family Link like compatible Android devices and Chromebooks.

### Should I read all of my child's messages?

Routine total surveillance can affect privacy and trust and is not required by every situation. Use transparent, proportionate controls based on age and risk, while escalating when there is a concrete safeguarding concern.

### What should I do after accidental exposure?

Stay calm, thank the child for telling you, ask enough to identify the route and any contact from another person, then adjust the control. Do not make accidental exposure a reason they are afraid to disclose the next event.

## Where to start

Check the account on the child's phone today. If it is an unrestricted adult or shared account, create the correct child account and enable the operating system's adult-website restriction before adding more products.

Then have the short conversation the filter cannot provide: if an upsetting or sexual image appears, close it and come to you without fear of punishment. Technology can reduce exposure, but that sentence keeps the route to help open.
